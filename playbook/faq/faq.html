Frequently asked questions

Ten questions people ask first

01

Is this replacing OCM practitioners?

No. The model rests on human in the loop: the advisors analyze, draft, and challenge; the OCM lead decides and delivers. Nothing reaches the client without the lead's judgment. The board gives a lead with three to five years of experience the specialist depth and second opinion they usually lack. It does not take their seat.

02

What do I need before I start?

A completed Client Change Context Brief: scope, timeline, populations, assumptions, and constraints. Every advisor works from it, so they don't contradict each other. If the Vision advisor assumes a six-month rollout and Training assumes twelve months phased, the strategy is incoherent. Then load the shared charter and the persona prompts.

Chapter 5 · page 16 · Appendix A · page 89
03

Which advisor should I use for my problem?

Start from each advisor's core question. "Why is this worth doing?" goes to Vision; "Who can block this?" to Stakeholders; "Can the business run at go-live?" to Readiness; "Are people actually adopting it?" to Analytics. If the question spans disciplines, take it to the Board Chair, which routes it to the right advisors.

Chapter 3 · page 11
04

How do the four operating modes differ, and where do I start?

  • Solo advisor: one expert drafts one deliverable.
  • Cross-functional: two to four advisors work a shared problem.
  • Board challenge: advisors independently give the strongest case for and against, and the evidence that would settle it.
  • End-to-end: the board guides you from discovery to sustained adoption.

Start with solo and widen the scope as your confidence grows.

05

What does the Board Chair do? Is it an eleventh advisor?

No. It is a coordinating function and the default mode you use to combine advice, settle conflicts between advisors, and prepare executive decisions. When Readiness says "not ready" and Hypercare says "launch with extra support," the Chair sets out both cases and names the decision the sponsor must make.

Chapter 8 · page 28
06

Can I trust what the board produces?

Only after you validate it. The charter forbids made-up facts, figures, baselines, and leader commitments. Advisors separate facts from assumptions and use [placeholders] when data is missing. Step 4 of the workflow, lead validates with real data, is required, and each advisor chapter ends with a quality checklist to run before anything goes to a client.

Chapters 5, 6, and 21 · pages 16, 22, and 80
07

What about client data and confidentiality?

Share only what you need, anonymize where you can, and follow your firm's and the client's policies, including privacy and data-residency requirements. Never paste sensitive personal data into prompts. Agree your firm's position on disclosing AI assistance to clients before any pilot.

Chapter 22 · page 84
08

What can't the board do?

With prompts alone there is no parallel execution, scheduled monitoring, automated handoffs, or persistent shared files. One conversation or custom assistant can simulate all four modes, but true orchestration needs an extra technology layer. The roadmap adds that layer only if the pilot shows it is worth the cost.

Chapters 22–23 · pages 84 and 86
09

How do we know whether it's working?

Test each advisor against eight scenarios: ambiguous request, incomplete data, conflicting priorities, executive deliverable, detailed working artifact, cross-advisor request, critical challenge, and follow-up changes. Then run cross-advisor tests for resistance, readiness gaps, and a difficult go-live. Score against three measures:

  • Quality: sound and defensible.
  • Consistency: compatible assumptions across advisors.
  • Practical value: faster, better decisions.

10

How do we roll this out, and what's being asked for now?

Five phases: Now define and configure, then test the specialists; Next test collaboration, then build reusable assets; Later orchestrate, only if the value justifies it. The 30-day ERP plan in Chapter 19 doubles as the pilot script.
