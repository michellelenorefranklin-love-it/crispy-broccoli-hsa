# 22. Limits, Data Protection, and Responsible Use

The board supports your judgment; it does not replace it. This chapter sets out what the board can and cannot do as a set of prompts, how to protect client data, how to verify outputs, what to tell clients, and the red lines you do not cross.

## What prompts alone can and cannot do

A persona prompt, used in one conversation or configured as a custom assistant, can simulate all four operating modes: solo advisor, cross-functional, board challenge, and end-to-end engagement. That is enough to get real value from the board today.

What prompts alone cannot provide is anything that requires work to happen outside the conversation you are in.

| Capability | Prompts alone | Needs orchestration or connected tools |
|---|---|---|
| One advisor drafting a deliverable | Yes | No |
| Several advisors responding in one conversation | Yes, simulated in sequence | No |
| Board challenge and Board Chair synthesis | Yes, simulated | No |
| Autonomous delegation between advisors | No | Yes |
| Parallel execution of advisor work | No | Yes |
| Persistent shared artifacts that every advisor reads and updates | No; you carry them between sessions | Yes |
| Automated handoffs, such as impact to training | No; you make each handoff | Yes |
| Scheduled monitoring, such as weekly adoption checks | No | Yes |

What this means in practice:

- **You are the orchestration layer.** You carry the Context Brief between conversations, paste the outputs one advisor hands to another, and decide when to rerun an analysis.
- **The board does not watch anything.** It will not notice that readiness scores dropped or that a sponsor changed position unless you tell it.
- **Simulated debate is still one model.** When several advisors "disagree" in one conversation, treat it as structured perspective-taking, not independent expert review. Real challenge from colleagues still matters.
- **Orchestration is a later decision.** Introduce it only if the value justifies it, as set out in phase 5 of the roadmap in Chapter 23.

## Protecting client data

Treat everything you put into the board as information leaving your direct control. Follow these rules on every engagement.

### Rules

1. **Follow client and firm policy first.** Your client contract, the client's information-security rules, and your firm's AI-use policy decide what you may share. When they are stricter than this chapter, they win. [Insert firm AI-use policy reference.]
2. **Use only approved tools.** Use the board only in environments your firm has approved for client work. [Insert approved tool list.]
3. **Minimize.** Share only what the advisor needs for the task. An impact assessment needs roles and process changes, not names and employee IDs.
4. **Anonymize.** Replace client names, people's names, and identifying details with roles or placeholders, such as "[Client]", "Regional Finance Director, [region]", or "[BU A]".
5. **Never share personal data about individuals** such as performance, health, compensation, or disciplinary information. Stakeholder analysis should describe roles and observed positions, not personal judgments about named people.
6. **Check applicable privacy and data-residency requirements** before sharing any client information, especially for multi-country programs. If you are unsure, ask your firm's privacy or risk contact before you proceed.
7. **Keep client-specific information separate from generic assets.** Templates, personas, and exemplar deliverables must contain no client data.
8. **Clean up.** Remove client material from conversations, custom assistants, and files at the end of the engagement, in line with policy.

> **Example:** Instead of pasting a stakeholder interview transcript, write: "Illustrative: Senior leader, [BU B], supportive of the goal but worried that the timeline conflicts with year-end close. Two of five regional managers interviewed raised the same concern." The advisor gets what it needs; the client's people stay protected.

## Verification practices

Board outputs are drafts until you verify them. Before anything reaches the client:

- **Trace every fact to a source.** If you cannot point to the client document, data set, or conversation it came from, it does not go in.
- **Check every number.** Baselines, targets, headcounts, dates, and scores must come from client data or be labeled as placeholders or illustrative.
- **Test assumptions with real people.** Persona traits, stakeholder positions, and impact ratings need validation with the people who know.
- **Check consistency against the Context Brief.** Confirm timeline, waves, scope, and audiences match across every deliverable.
- **Read for invented commitments.** Remove any statement that implies a leader has agreed to something they have not.
- **Check external claims.** Methods, standards, or regulations the board mentions must be confirmed from authoritative sources before you rely on them.
- **Apply your own judgment.** Ask whether you would defend this advice to the sponsor without mentioning the board. If not, it is not ready.

## Disclosure to clients

Decide how you will describe your use of the board before the engagement starts, not when a client asks.

[Insert firm policy on disclosing AI assistance to clients.]

Until firm policy is in place, apply these working principles:

- Be honest if asked. Describe the board accurately as an AI-assisted advisory tool that helps you analyze and draft.
- Make clear that you validate every output and that you own the advice.
- Confirm that client data is handled according to the client's and the firm's requirements.
- Follow any client contract terms that restrict or require notice of AI use.

## Red lines

Do not do any of the following, regardless of time pressure:

- Send board output to a client without your own review and validation.
- Present an assumption, illustrative persona, or placeholder figure as fact.
- Share client data in an unapproved tool or beyond what policy allows.
- Use the board to profile or make judgments about named individuals.
- Let the board make, or appear to make, decisions that belong to you, the sponsor, or the client.
- Cite the board as the authority for advice. You are accountable for what you recommend.
- Use the board to replace required human expertise, such as legal, HR, works-council, or security review.

> **Watch out:** The most common breach is not deliberate. It is pasting a "quick" email thread or spreadsheet under deadline pressure. Build anonymization into your routine so the safe path is also the fast one.
