# Appendix D. Glossary

Terms are listed alphabetically. Chapter references point to where each term is used most.

**Adoption.** The point at which people use the change as intended, correctly and independently, as part of normal work. Logins or attendance alone do not prove adoption (Chapter 18).

**Advisor.** One of the ten specialist personas on the board, each configured with the eight elements and the shared charter (Chapters 3 and 4).

**Board Chair.** The Chief Change Strategist function. It routes requests, synthesizes advice, resolves conflicts between advisors, and prepares executive decisions. It is the default operating mode, not an eleventh specialist (Chapter 8).

**Board Operating Charter.** The shared set of principles, collaboration rules, and decision support that every advisor follows. It turns a persona library into a coherent board (Chapter 5, Appendix A).

**Case for change.** A short argument for why the change is needed, why now, and why each group should back it. Owned by Advisor 1 (Chapter 9).

**Champion network.** A group of credible local change agents who support adoption in their area. Champions do not replace managers, training, or the help desk (Chapter 14).

**Change load.** The cumulative amount of change a group absorbs at the same time, across this and other initiatives. Used to spot overload (Chapter 13).

**Client Change Context Brief.** The single shared record of scope, timeline, assumptions, and constraints for an engagement. It is the foundation layer of the guardrails, and new learning returns to it (Chapter 5).

**Decision lens.** Element 4 of a persona: the question an advisor uses to judge options (Chapter 4).

**Eight elements.** The standard anatomy of an advisor: professional identity, mission, scope and boundaries, decision-making lens, core capabilities, deliverables, personality and challenge style, and operating rules (Chapter 4).

**Exit criteria.** The conditions that must be met before hypercare ends and support moves to business as usual (Chapter 17).

**Go/no-go.** A formal decision on whether to proceed with a launch, wave, or scale-up, made against agreed criteria. The client decides; the OCM lead recommends (Chapters 12 and 17).

**Guardrails.** The three layers that keep the board coherent: the Context Brief, the Board Operating Charter, and the standard response format (Chapter 5).

**Human in the loop.** The operating model in which the board analyzes, drafts, and challenges, and the OCM lead decides and delivers. Nothing reaches the client without the lead's judgment (Chapter 2).

**Hypercare.** A defined period of heightened support after go-live, used to detect friction quickly and stabilize adoption. IT owns incident management within it (Chapter 17).

**Hypothesis.** A statement the board believes may be true but has not been validated. It must be labeled and tested before use (Chapter 5).

**Impact assessment.** A traceable view of what changes across people, process, technology, roles, and behaviors, and who is affected. Impacts, risks, and issues are kept distinct (Chapter 13).

**Influence–interest grid.** A matrix that positions stakeholders by their influence over the change and their interest in it, to guide engagement (Chapter 10).

**Lagging indicator.** A measure that confirms an outcome after it has happened, such as process performance after go-live (Chapter 18).

**Leading indicator.** A measure that signals likely future outcomes early enough to act, such as demonstrated proficiency before go-live (Chapter 18).

**Lifecycle stages.** The five stages used in the Context Brief: discovery and assessment; strategy and design; build and preparation; pilot and deployment; and hypercare and sustained adoption (Chapter 20).

**OCM lead.** The human change management practitioner who frames problems, validates outputs with real data, decides, and delivers to the client. Always accountable (Chapter 2).

**Operating modes.** The four ways to use the board: solo advisor, cross-functional, board challenge, and end-to-end engagement (Chapter 7).

**Orchestration layer.** Software or connected tools that add autonomous delegation, parallel execution, persistent shared artifacts, automated handoffs, or scheduled monitoring. Prompts alone do not provide these (Chapters 22 and 23).

**Persona.** The configured prompt that defines an advisor's identity, scope, and behavior (Appendix A).

**Pilot.** A limited, early deployment used to reduce uncertainty before scale-up, with defined objectives and success criteria (Chapter 17).

**RACI.** A matrix assigning Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed roles. The OCM lead is Accountable; the board never is (Appendix C).

**Readiness.** Demonstrated capability of a business unit, country, or wave to operate at go-live and beyond. Training completion or a positive survey is not readiness (Chapter 12).

**Readiness heatmap.** A visual summary of readiness scores by business unit, country, or deployment wave against agreed criteria and thresholds (Chapter 12).

**Resistance.** Concern or opposition to the change. The board treats it as information to understand, not as irrational behavior (Chapter 10).

**Response format.** The nine standard sections advisors use: Recommendation, Rationale, Analysis, Deliverable, Risks, Decisions required, Success measures, Next actions, and Board consultation. Include only those that add value (Chapter 5).

**Risk register.** A log of potential events that could harm the change, with likelihood, impact, owner, and mitigation (Chapter 13).

**Sponsor.** The client executive who owns the change, approves key decisions, and visibly champions it (Chapter 10).

**Train-the-trainer.** An approach in which selected people are trained to deliver training to others, often used for scale and localization (Chapter 16).

**Vanity metric.** A number that looks positive but does not indicate adoption or trigger action, such as raw page views (Chapter 18).

**WIIFM.** "What's in it for me": a statement of the specific benefit a group gains from the change, written from that group's point of view (Chapter 9).

**Wave.** A defined group of business units, countries, or users that goes live together in a phased deployment (Chapter 12).
