# 6. The Five-Step Workflow

The board works through one repeatable loop: you frame the problem, the board advises and drafts, and you decide and deliver. The five steps below turn that loop into actions you can run for any request.

| Step | Owner | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Lead shares context | Human | Context Brief, priorities, constraints, real client data |
| 2. Chair routes to advisors | Board | The Board Chair picks lead and contributing advisors |
| 3. Advisors respond in standard format | Board | Recommendations, drafts, challenge, risks, executive-ready language |
| 4. Lead validates with real data | Human | You test output against facts and real stakeholders |
| 5. Lead delivers to client | Human | You refine, present to client and sponsor, and own the result |

A feedback loop returns new learning to the Context Brief. Humans own judgment, client relationships, and decisions; the board accelerates analysis and strengthens thinking. Three of the five steps are yours.

## Step 1: Lead shares context

State the outcome you need, the audience, and the decision it supports. Reference the current Context Brief version (Chapter 5) and say whether the board should ask questions first or proceed on labeled assumptions.

A weak request: "Write a comms plan." A framed request: "Using Context Brief v[1.2], draft a communications approach for wave 1 finance users in [countries]. The sponsor reviews it on [date]. Priority is manager cascade. Constraint: no approved brand standards yet. Ask me up to three questions before drafting."

- [ ] The brief version is current and referenced.
- [ ] Outcome, audience, decision, priorities, and constraints are stated.
- [ ] Shared data is anonymized or summarized as your data rules require.

> **Tip:** The board's advice is capped by your framing. If you cannot state the decision the output supports, work that out first.

## Step 2: Chair routes to advisors

The Board Chair names a lead advisor, any contributors with a specific question each, and the operating mode (Chapter 7). For simple requests, route yourself by naming the advisor. Use the lifecycle stage as a starting point:

| Lifecycle stage | Indicative lead advisors |
|---|---|
| Discovery and assessment | 1–5 |
| Strategy and design | 4, 6–8, 10 |
| Build and preparation | 6–8 |
| Pilot and deployment | 4, 9 |
| Hypercare and sustained adoption | 9, 10 |

Every advisor can be called at any stage. Limit contributors to those whose discipline changes the answer, and start with one advisor if unsure.

## Step 3: Advisors respond in standard format

Each advisor works within its scope and uses the nine-section format, including only sections that add value. Where advisors work together, the Chair synthesizes and surfaces conflicts rather than hiding them. Check the response:

- [ ] The recommendation comes first, stated as an action.
- [ ] Assumptions are labeled and none contradict the brief.
- [ ] No invented figures, scores, names, or commitments; placeholders mark missing data.
- [ ] Risks and decisions are client-specific; Board consultation names the right handoffs.

If an item fails, send it back. That is normal iteration, not a malfunction.

## Step 4: Lead validates with real data

Test every claim that matters against client data, documents, stakeholders, and subject-matter experts. Confirm or replace each labeled assumption, check consistency with other deliverables, and decide what you accept, reject, or change.

| Technique | How to apply it | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Assumption trace | Mark each assumption Confirmed, Replaced, or Still open, with a source | Any output |
| Stakeholder test | Walk two or three real stakeholders through the draft | Personas, journey maps, messaging |

Other useful checks: spot-check data against client sources, compare assumptions with your other deliverables, ask the board for the strongest case against its own recommendation, and have an SME review specialist content.

- [ ] Every number has a real source or is marked illustrative or placeholder.
- [ ] A real stakeholder has seen any people-facing artifact.
- [ ] You can defend the recommendation in your own words.

> **Watch out:** Validation is the step most often skipped under time pressure, and skipping it is how invented context reaches a client. If you cannot validate a section in time, label it "draft for discussion, assumptions unconfirmed."

## Step 5: Lead delivers to client

Make the output your own: adjust tone, apply client templates, and add context only you know. Present it, handle pushback yourself, and record decisions with owner and date. Check that nothing commits an executive to something they have not agreed, and capture new facts for the feedback loop.

## The feedback loop and Context Brief versioning

Every cycle yields new learning: a confirmed timeline, a stakeholder concern, a sponsor decision. It returns to the Context Brief so the next request starts from it.

| Change type | Version step | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Correction, or assumption confirmed or replaced | Minor (v1.1 to v1.2), with register entry | Wave 1 scope confirmed by steering committee |
| Change to scope, timeline, or deployment approach | Major (v1.x to v2.0) | Sponsor changes go-live date |

After every major version, log the change with date and source, update the assumptions register, ask the board which outputs are affected, and re-issue them noting the brief version.

> **Tip:** Put the brief version in the footer of every board-assisted deliverable, so you can find affected documents in minutes.

## Worked mini-walkthrough

This uses the fictional global ERP client; all details are illustrative.

**Step 1.** You share Context Brief v1.0 (phased deployment, dates TBC, limited visibility into impacts) and ask for a change impact assessment approach for finance, with up to three questions first.

**Step 2.** The Chair routes to the Change Impact, Risk & Mitigation Architect (Advisor 5), naming Advisors 8 and 4 for consultation because impacts feed training and readiness.

**Step 3.** Advisor 5 asks about wave 1 processes, process maps, and role redesign; you say role redesign is unknown. It returns a template with placeholders, a starter risk register, a labeled assumption that no roles are redesigned, and a note asking Advisor 8 to map impacts to training needs.

**Step 4.** The finance process owner tells you two roles are being consolidated. You replace the assumption.

**Step 5.** You present the adapted approach to the finance lead, who agrees to nominate workshop participants by [date].

**Feedback loop.** Role consolidation is a scope change, so you issue v2.0. Advisor 5 updates the template; Advisor 8 adds the consolidated roles to the training needs analysis.

The board analyzed and drafted. You framed, validated, decided, and delivered.
