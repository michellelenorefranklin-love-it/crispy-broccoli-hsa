# 6. The Five-Step Workflow

The board works through one repeatable loop. You frame the problem, the board advises and drafts, and you decide and deliver. The five steps below break that loop into actions you can run every time, whether the request takes ten minutes or two weeks.

| Step | Owner | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Lead shares context | Human | You give the board the Context Brief, priorities, constraints, and real client data |
| 2. Chair routes to advisors | Board | The Board Chair function decides which advisors lead and which contribute |
| 3. Advisors respond in standard format | Board | Advisors produce recommendations, drafts, challenge, risks, and executive-ready language |
| 4. Lead validates with real data | Human | You test the output against facts and real stakeholders |
| 5. Lead delivers to client | Human | You refine, present to client and sponsor, and own the result |

A feedback loop connects step 5 back to step 1: whatever you learn returns to the Context Brief, so the next request starts from better information.

The division is deliberate. Humans own judgment, client relationships, and decisions. The board accelerates analysis and strengthens thinking. Three of the five steps are yours.

## Step 1: Lead shares context

**Inputs.** The current Client Change Context Brief (Chapter 5), your priorities for this request, the constraints you are working within, and any real client data you are allowed to share.

**Actions.** Write a request that states the outcome you need, the audience for the output, and the decision it supports. Attach or reference the brief version. Say whether you want the board to ask questions first or to proceed on labeled assumptions.

**Outputs.** A framed request the board can act on without guessing.

A weak request: "Write a comms plan." A framed request: "Using Context Brief v[1.2], draft a communications approach for wave 1 finance users in [countries]. The sponsor reviews it on [date]. Priority is manager cascade. Constraint: no approved brand standards yet. Ask me up to three questions before drafting."

**Step 1 checklist**

- [ ] The brief version is current and attached or referenced.
- [ ] The request names the outcome, the audience, and the decision it supports.
- [ ] Priorities and constraints are stated, not implied.
- [ ] Shared data is anonymized or summarized as your data rules require.
- [ ] You have said whether to ask questions first or proceed on assumptions.

> **Tip:** The quality of the board's advice is capped by the quality of your framing. If you cannot state the decision the output supports, pause and work that out first.

## Step 2: Chair routes to advisors

**Inputs.** Your framed request.

**Actions.** The Board Chair function identifies which disciplines the request touches, names a lead advisor, and names any contributing advisors. It chooses the operating mode (Chapter 7): solo, cross-functional, board challenge, or end-to-end. For simple requests you can route yourself by naming the advisor directly.

**Outputs.** A routing decision: lead advisor, contributors, mode, and the specific question each advisor answers.

Use the lifecycle stage as a starting point for routing:

| Lifecycle stage | Indicative lead advisors |
|---|---|
| Discovery and assessment | 1–5 |
| Strategy and design | 4, 6–8, 10 |
| Build and preparation | 6–8 |
| Pilot and deployment | 4, 9 |
| Hypercare and sustained adoption | 9, 10 |

Every advisor can be called at any stage. The table tells you where to start, not where to stop.

**Step 2 checklist**

- [ ] One advisor is named as lead, so accountability for the draft is clear.
- [ ] Contributors are limited to those whose discipline changes the answer.
- [ ] Each contributor has a specific question, not a general invitation.
- [ ] The mode fits the request (start with one advisor if unsure).

## Step 3: Advisors respond in standard format

**Inputs.** The routed request, the Context Brief, and the Charter.

**Actions.** Each advisor works within its scope and responds using the nine-section format, including only the sections that add value. Where advisors are working together, the Chair synthesizes their views and surfaces any conflicts rather than hiding them.

**Outputs.** What the board contributes: recommendations, first-draft deliverables, challenge, risks, and executive-ready language.

**Step 3 checklist (what to look for in the response)**

- [ ] The recommendation comes first and is stated as an action.
- [ ] Assumptions are labeled, and none contradict the Context Brief.
- [ ] No invented figures, scores, names, quotes, or commitments appear.
- [ ] Placeholders mark every place where real data is needed.
- [ ] Risks and decisions required are specific to this client.
- [ ] Board consultation names the right advisors for handoffs.

If any item fails, send it back. Ask the advisor to label the assumption, remove the invented figure, or make the risk specific. Treat this as normal iteration, not a malfunction.

## Step 4: Lead validates with real data

**Inputs.** The board's output and whatever real evidence you can reach: client data, documents, stakeholder conversations, subject-matter experts.

**Actions.** Test every claim that matters against reality. Confirm or replace each labeled assumption. Check the output against other deliverables for consistency. Decide what you agree with, what you reject, and what you change.

**Outputs.** A validated draft and an updated list of confirmed facts and replaced assumptions.

### Validation techniques

| Technique | How to apply it | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Assumption trace | List every labeled assumption; mark each Confirmed, Replaced, or Still open with a source | Any output |
| Data spot-check | Compare figures, segments, and scope against client source documents | Impact assessments, readiness baselines, dashboards |
| Stakeholder test | Walk two or three real stakeholders through the draft and note where it does not match their reality | Personas, journey maps, stakeholder maps, messaging |
| Consistency check | Compare timeline, scope, and population assumptions against your other current deliverables | Any deliverable feeding another |
| Red-team request | Ask the board for the strongest case against its own recommendation | Go/no-go, executive recommendations |
| SME review | Ask a process, system, or HR expert to check specialist content | Training needs, process impacts, legal or works council items |
| Proportion check | Ask whether the effort implied by the plan fits the client's capacity and budget | Champion networks, comms cadence, training plans |

**Step 4 checklist**

- [ ] Every assumption that changes the recommendation is confirmed or replaced.
- [ ] Every number in the output has a real source or is clearly marked illustrative or placeholder.
- [ ] At least one real stakeholder has seen any people-facing artifact.
- [ ] The output is consistent with your other current deliverables.
- [ ] You can explain and defend the recommendation in your own words.

> **Watch out:** Validation is the step most often skipped under time pressure. Skipping it is how invented context reaches a client. If you cannot validate a section in time, label it "draft for discussion, assumptions unconfirmed" before it goes anywhere.

## Step 5: Lead delivers to client

**Inputs.** The validated draft.

**Actions.** Refine the output into your own work: adjust tone for the audience, apply client templates and standards, and add the context only you know. Present it to the client and sponsor. Handle questions and pushback yourself. Record decisions made.

**Outputs.** A client-ready deliverable, client decisions, and new learning.

**Step 5 checklist**

- [ ] The deliverable is in the client's format and voice, not the board's.
- [ ] You have prepared for the two or three hardest questions the sponsor may ask.
- [ ] Decisions taken in the meeting are recorded with owner and date.
- [ ] Nothing in the deliverable commits an executive to something they have not agreed.
- [ ] New facts and decisions are captured for the feedback loop.

## The feedback loop and Context Brief versioning

Every cycle produces new learning: a confirmed timeline, a stakeholder concern, a readiness gap, a sponsor decision. That learning returns to the Context Brief, and the next request starts from it. Without the loop, the board keeps advising on version 1.0 of an engagement that has moved on.

### Versioning rules

| Change type | Version step | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Correction or minor clarification | Minor (v1.1 to v1.2) | Spelling of a business unit; adding a known contact role |
| Assumption confirmed or replaced | Minor, with register entry | Wave 1 scope confirmed by steering committee |
| Change to scope, timeline, or deployment approach | Major (v1.x to v2.0) | Rollout moves from big bang to phased |
| New client decision affecting several deliverables | Major | Sponsor changes go-live date |

### After every major version

1. Record the change in the brief's change log with date and source.
2. Update the assumptions register.
3. Tell the board what changed and ask which existing outputs are affected. The Charter requires advisors to update outputs consistently when scope changes.
4. Re-issue affected deliverables, and note the brief version each one is based on.

> **Tip:** Put the brief version in the footer of every deliverable the board helped draft. When the brief changes, you can find every affected document in minutes.

## Worked mini-walkthrough

This walkthrough uses the fictional global ERP client. All details are illustrative.

**Step 1.** You share Context Brief v1.0. Deployment is phased, dates are TBC, and the known challenge is limited visibility into impacts. You ask: "Draft a change impact assessment approach for the finance function. Ask up to three questions first."

**Step 2.** The Chair routes to the Change Impact, Risk & Mitigation Architect (Advisor 5) as lead, with the Learning Strategy & Capability Development Architect (Advisor 8) and the Business Readiness & Operational Transition Expert (Advisor 4) named for consultation, because impacts feed both training and readiness. Mode: solo advisor with board consultation.

**Step 3.** Advisor 5 asks which finance processes are in wave 1, whether process maps exist, and whether any roles are being redesigned. You answer the first two and say role redesign is unknown. Advisor 5 returns a recommendation, an impact assessment template with placeholders, a starter risk register, a labeled assumption that no roles are redesigned in wave 1, and a board consultation note asking Advisor 8 to map impacts to training needs.

**Step 4.** You check the assumption with the finance process owner. Two roles are, in fact, being consolidated. You replace the assumption and spot-check the process list against the client's process inventory.

**Step 5.** You adapt the template to the client's format and present the approach to the finance lead, who agrees to nominate impact workshop participants by [date].

**Feedback loop.** Role consolidation is a scope change. You issue Context Brief v2.0, log the replaced assumption, and ask the board which outputs it affects. Advisor 5 updates the impact template, and Advisor 8 flags that the training needs analysis must include the consolidated roles.

The board did the analysis and drafting. You framed, validated, decided, and delivered.
