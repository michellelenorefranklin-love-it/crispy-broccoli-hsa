# How to Use This Playbook

This playbook turns the executive overview *The Agentic Board of Organizational Change Advisors* into an operating manual. The overview explained the idea: ten specialist AI advisor personas, a Board Chair function, and a shared operating charter that together coach an organizational change management (OCM) lead through every change discipline and deliverable. This playbook explains how to run it, day to day, on a real engagement.

One principle runs through every page: **the OCM lead is the human in the loop.** The advisors analyze, draft, and challenge. The OCM lead decides and delivers. Nothing reaches the client without the lead's judgment.

## Who it is for

- **OCM leads with three to five years of experience** who are asked to deliver across vision, stakeholders, experience, readiness, impact, champions, communications, training, hypercare, and measurement, usually without a specialist for each beside them.
- **OCM directors and practice leaders** who want consistent, defensible change work across a team, and a faster path from capable practitioner to trusted executive advisor.
- **Engagement and program leaders** who want to understand what the board does, what it does not do, and where human accountability sits.

## How it is organized

| Part | What it covers | Read it when |
|---|---|---|
| I. Foundations (Chapters 1–4) | Why a board, the human-in-the-loop model, the board's architecture, and the anatomy of an advisor | You are new to the board or explaining it to others |
| II. Operating the Board (Chapters 5–8) | The Context Brief, the Operating Charter, the response format, the five-step workflow, the four operating modes, and the Board Chair | You are setting up the board for an engagement |
| III. The Advisor Playbooks (Chapters 9–18) | One chapter per advisor: when to call them, what to give them, how they work, their deliverables, and how they will challenge you | You need a specific deliverable or a second opinion in one discipline |
| IV. Applying the Board (Chapters 19–20) | The first 30 days of a global ERP program, and plays for each lifecycle stage | You are starting or running an engagement |
| V. Quality and Responsible Use (Chapters 21–22) | Testing personas, scoring outputs, limits, and data protection | You are piloting the board or reviewing its outputs |
| VI. Implementation (Chapter 23) | The five-phase roadmap and a pilot plan | You are deciding whether and how to adopt the board |
| Appendices A–D | Configuration prompts, a prompt library, a deliverables index and RACI, and a glossary | You need something to copy, paste, or look up |

## Three ways to read it

1. **The fast start (one hour).** Read Chapters 1, 2, 5, and 7, then pick one advisor chapter from Part III and try its prompts on a fictional scenario.
2. **The engagement start (one day).** Complete the Client Change Context Brief in Chapter 5, configure the board from Appendix A, and follow Chapter 19 as your plan for the first month.
3. **The reference.** Keep Part III and the appendices open while you work. Each advisor chapter follows the same structure, so you always know where to find inputs, templates, challenge questions, and quality checks.

## Conventions used

- **Bracketed text** such as `[target %]`, `[BU name]`, or `[date]` marks a placeholder you must fill with validated client facts. The board never fills these in by guessing, and neither should you.
- **Illustrative examples** use a fictional global enterprise running a phased ERP implementation across multiple business units and countries. Names, figures, and findings in those examples are invented for teaching and are labeled as illustrative.
- **Prompts** in shaded boxes are ready to copy. Replace the placeholders and attach your Context Brief before you use them.
- **Callouts** marked *Tip*, *Watch out*, and *Example* highlight practical advice, common failure modes, and worked illustrations.

## What this playbook is not

It is not a replacement for a change methodology, for client policies, or for professional judgment. It does not claim research findings or benchmarks it cannot evidence. And it does not turn the board into an autonomous system: prompts alone give you a disciplined thinking partner, not parallel execution or scheduled monitoring. Chapter 22 is explicit about those limits.

> **Tip:** If you only remember one thing, remember the sequence: *the lead frames, the board advises and drafts, the lead decides and delivers.*
