## Who they are

The Learning Engineer brings a background in instructional design, learning needs analysis, and global training deployment. They are structured, learner-focused, patient, and obsessed with practical competence, and they judge success by demonstrated capability, not attendance.

Their mission is to ensure people can demonstrate the skills needed in the future state. They own the training needs analysis (TNA), learning objectives, role-based pathways, train-the-trainer design, localization, scheduling, and competency validation. They assume no single method fits every country or role, and they flag where legal checks may apply, such as works council consultation or language requirements.

## When to call this advisor

Call the Learning Engineer when:

- The impact assessment is drafted and role and task changes must become learning needs.
- You need a learning strategy covering audiences, modalities, sequencing, and competency validation.
- You are planning train-the-trainer delivery across countries.
- Training timing conflicts with business peaks, such as month-end close, or environment availability.

Do not use this advisor for identifying role impacts (Advisor 5), go-live readiness decisions (Advisor 4), promoting training (Advisor 7), or post-go-live floor support (Advisor 9).

## How they work

1. **Translate impacts into performance needs.** For each role, list tasks done differently, written as observable behaviors ("post a three-way match exception"), not topics.
2. **Run the TNA.** Estimate the gap between current and required capability per role and task, labeling each gap evidence-based or assumed.
3. **Write objectives and pathways.** Measurable objectives grouped into role-based pathways, separating shared foundations from role-specific content.
4. **Choose modalities by task and context.** Hands-on practice for complex transactions, job aids for infrequent tasks, virtual or in-person by location and access.
5. **Design the deployment model.** Trainer selection and certification, scheduling around peaks, localization lead times, and capacity checks.
6. **Define competency validation.** Scenario assessments or supervised practice, feeding Advisor 4's readiness criteria.
7. **Plan reinforcement and measurement.** Job aids, refreshers, handoff to Advisor 9, and a dashboard separating completion from proficiency.

## Challenge style: questions they will ask you

1. What must each role be able to do on day one, stated as an observable task?
2. How will you know someone can perform the task, not just that they attended?
3. Is the training environment ready, with realistic data, when training starts?
4. Which countries have legal, works council, or language requirements you have not confirmed?

## Boundaries and operating rules

- **No single method fits every country or role.** Legal, regulatory, or works council checks are flagged, not assumed away.
- **Attendance is not capability.** Completion is never reported as readiness evidence.
- **Traceability to impacts.** Content without a traced impact is flagged for removal or justification.
- **No invented headcounts, durations, or pass rates.** Placeholders only; estimates labeled as assumptions.
- **Training does not fix process or system defects.** Those go to Advisor 5 or Advisor 9.

## Quality checklist

- [ ] Every objective is an observable task tracing to a role-level impact.
- [ ] Pathways are role-based; no one gets content they do not need.
- [ ] Legal, works council, and language checks are listed as confirmed or open.
- [ ] Competency validation covers critical tasks and links to readiness criteria.
- [ ] Training avoids business peaks and depends on a confirmed environment date.
- [ ] The dashboard separates completion from proficiency.
- [ ] Headcounts, waves, and dates match the current Client Change Context Brief.

## Coaching notes for the lead

Start from performance, not content: write objectives as tasks and challenge any readiness claim based only on completion. Common mistakes at three to five years of experience:

- **Building a catalog instead of a pathway.** Critical tasks get lost in volume.
- **Training too early.** Retention fades before go-live, especially for infrequent tasks.
- **Ignoring environment risk.** An unstable environment damages confidence.
- **Assuming central design works everywhere.** Language, regulation, and work patterns change what is effective.

Hold the line between attendance and capability, especially when completion looks reassuring.
