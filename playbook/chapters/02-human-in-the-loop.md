# 2. The Human-in-the-Loop Operating Model

The operating idea behind the board fits in one line: **OCM Lead – Human in the loop.** The advisors analyze, draft, and challenge. You decide and deliver. Nothing reaches the client without your judgment.

This chapter explains how that division of labor works in practice: what flows between you and the board, the three-step rhythm of every interaction, who holds which decision rights, what you must never delegate, how to tell when you are leaning on the board too much, and how to use it to grow your own capability.

## Two roles, one engagement

The board and the lead do different work. Keep the line between them sharp.

| The board | The OCM lead |
|---|---|
| **Analyzes** context, data you provide, and options | **Decides** which recommendation to adopt, adapt, or reject |
| **Drafts** first versions of deliverables in a standard format | **Delivers** refined work to the client and sponsor |
| **Challenges** your reasoning, assumptions, and evidence | Owns **judgment**, **client relationships**, and **decisions** |

Humans own judgment, client relationships, and decisions. The board accelerates analysis and strengthens thinking. If you find the board doing anything in the right-hand column, stop and reset.

> **Tip:** Ask yourself, "Who made the call?" If the honest answer is "the board," you have crossed the line.

## The two-way flow

Advice flows from the board to you. Decisions, direction, and validated facts flow from you back to the board.

### What flows to you

- Recommendations, with rationale and labeled assumptions
- First-draft deliverables
- Challenge: questions, counterarguments, weak points in the evidence
- Risks and the decisions they require
- Executive-ready language you can adapt

### What flows back to the board

- **Decisions:** "We are going with option B. Update the training plan to match."
- **Direction:** "Focus on the finance function first. Deprioritize the warehouse population for now."
- **Validated facts:** "The sponsor confirmed three deployment waves, not two. Wave 1 is [BU name] in [country]."

The second flow is what keeps the board useful over time. If you take advice but never return decisions and facts, every advisor keeps working from assumptions, and outputs drift away from the real engagement. New learning goes back into the Client Change Context Brief so every advisor works from the same, current picture. Chapter 6, "The Five-Step Workflow," covers how to version the Brief.

> **Watch out:** Validated facts must come from real sources: client data, stakeholder conversations, sponsor confirmation. A fact the board suggested and you did not check is still an assumption.

## The three steps

Every interaction with the board follows the same rhythm.

### Step 1: The human frames the problem

You set the question, the context, and the constraints. Good framing is the single biggest factor in the quality of what comes back.

**Include:**

- The specific question or deliverable you need
- The relevant part of the Context Brief (scope, timeline, populations, waves)
- Constraints: client preferences, politics, budget, timing
- What you already know and what you do not
- Who the output is for (you, your team, the sponsor, a steering committee)

```text
Context: [paste relevant Context Brief fields].
Question: I need a first-draft stakeholder map for the [program name] ERP program, focused on the finance function.
Known: [list confirmed sponsors and leaders].
Unknown: informal influencers, union or works council positions in [country].
Audience: my working draft, not client-facing yet.
Please label every assumption and ask me up to five questions before you draft.
```

### Step 2: The board advises and drafts

The Board Chair routes your request to the right advisors, who respond in the standard format: Recommendation, Rationale, Analysis, Deliverable, Risks, Decisions required, Success measures, Next actions, Board consultation. They include only the sections that add value.

**What you should see:** targeted questions or clearly labeled assumptions, trade-offs surfaced rather than hidden, and no invented facts or scores.

### Step 3: The human decides and delivers

You validate the output against real data and real stakeholders, decide what to keep, refine the work, and present it to the client and sponsor in your own voice.

**Before you deliver, check:**

- [ ] Every assumption is either validated or still clearly labeled.
- [ ] No numbers, names, commitments, or quotes came from the board without a real source.
- [ ] The output is consistent with other deliverables and the current Context Brief.
- [ ] You can defend every recommendation without saying "the board said so."
- [ ] Decisions and new facts have been fed back to the board.

## Decision rights

Use this table to settle who decides what. Where the table says "advises," the board's role ends at recommendation.

| Decision | OCM lead | Board | Client / sponsor |
|---|---|---|---|
| How to frame the problem and which advisors to consult | Decides | Advises (Board Chair suggests routing) | Not involved |
| Which assumptions to accept as working hypotheses | Decides | Proposes and labels | Confirms where needed |
| Content of draft deliverables | Decides and refines | Drafts | Reviews |
| Whether a deliverable is ready for the client | Decides | Challenges | Not involved |
| Stakeholder engagement approach | Recommends | Advises | Approves |
| Readiness rating and go/no-go recommendation | Recommends | Analyzes and challenges | Decides |
| Business case, benefits, and targets | Facilitates | Structures and challenges | Owns and decides |
| Leader messages and commitments | Drafts and recommends | Drafts in provisional voice | Approves |
| Scope, timeline, and deployment waves | Validates and records | Flags inconsistencies | Decides |
| What client data the board may see | Decides, within client policy | Not involved | Sets policy |

Notice that the board never holds a "decides" in any row. Notice also that on the highest-stakes decisions, such as go/no-go, benefits, and scope, you recommend and the client decides. The board's job is to make your recommendation stronger.

## What never gets delegated

Some work stays with you on every engagement, no matter how good the board's draft is.

1. **Final judgment on recommendations.** The board can present the strongest case for and against. Only you choose.
2. **Client relationships.** Conversations with sponsors, leaders, and employees are yours. The board can help you prepare; it cannot hold the relationship.
3. **Validation with real stakeholders.** Personas, stakeholder maps, and impact assessments remain hypotheses until real people confirm them.
4. **Facts about the client.** Baselines, financial benefits, readiness scores, survey results, and commitments must come from the client, not from the board.
5. **What reaches the client.** Every document, message, and slide passes through your review.
6. **Decisions about client data.** You decide what information the board sees, within the client's data policies. Protect client data.
7. **Ethical and political calls.** Judgments about how to handle resistance, sensitive groups, or leadership conflict require context the board does not have.

> **Watch out:** The most common slip is not handing over a decision outright. It is accepting a board draft with an unlabeled assumption buried in it and presenting it as fact. Read every draft as if a skeptical sponsor will ask, "Where did this come from?"

## Signs you are over-relying on the board

Over-reliance creeps in under deadline pressure. Review this list weekly.

| Sign | What it suggests | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| You send board drafts to the client with light edits | You have stopped applying your own judgment | Rewrite the summary and recommendation in your own words before sending |
| You cannot explain a recommendation without rereading the output | You have not internalized the reasoning | Ask the advisor to walk through its rationale, then explain it back |
| Facts in your deliverables cannot be traced to a client source | Board assumptions have become "facts" | Audit the deliverable and relabel or validate each claim |
| You consult the board before forming any view of your own | Your own thinking is not developing | Write your view first, then ask the board to challenge it |
| You skip stakeholder conversations because the board's map "looks right" | Validation has been dropped | Schedule the conversations; treat the map as a hypothesis |
| The Context Brief has not been updated in weeks | The feedback loop has stopped | Update the Brief with every decision and validated fact |

If two or more of these apply, pause and rebalance before your next client deliverable.

## Professional growth through the board

The board is designed to make you better, not just faster. Faster professional growth for the lead is one of its three core outcomes. That growth does not happen automatically; you need to use the board deliberately.

### Practices that build capability

- **Form a view first.** Before you ask an advisor for a recommendation, write your own in two or three sentences. Compare it with the advisor's. The gaps are your learning.
- **Ask for the reasoning, not only the answer.** "What would make you change this recommendation?" teaches you the advisor's decision-making lens.
- **Study the challenge questions.** Each advisor has a characteristic challenge style. The Operational Realist doubts unsupported green status. The Commercial Challenger pushes from activity to outcomes. Learn to ask those questions of your own work before the board does.
- **Rehearse executive moments.** Use the Board Chair to prepare decision framing, then present it to the board and ask for the hardest sponsor questions.
- **Work in your weaker disciplines on purpose.** Use the self-rating from Chapter 1 and spend time with the advisors for disciplines you marked "working knowledge" or "limited."
- **Keep a short learning log.** After each significant interaction, note one thing the advisor saw that you missed.

### A simple growth plan

| Period | Focus | Practice |
|---|---|---|
| First [2–4] weeks | Learn the board | Use one advisor at a time in solo mode on your strongest discipline, so you can judge output quality |
| Next [4–8] weeks | Fill gaps | Work with advisors in your weaker disciplines; form a view first every time |
| Ongoing | Build judgment | Use board challenge mode on your own recommendations before every executive decision point |

Share the learning log with your director in development conversations.

> **Example:** On a fictional global ERP program, a lead drafts a readiness recommendation of "ready" for Wave 1 based on training completion. Before sending it, she asks the Business Readiness & Operational Transition Expert to challenge it. The advisor notes that training completion is not proof of readiness and asks what demonstrated capability exists. The lead adds a requirement for [simulation or dress-rehearsal results] to her go/no-go criteria. The next time, she asks that question herself before consulting the board.
