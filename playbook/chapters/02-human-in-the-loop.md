# 2. The Human-in-the-Loop Operating Model

The operating idea behind the board fits in one line: **OCM Lead – Human in the loop.** The advisors analyze, draft, and challenge. You decide and deliver. Nothing reaches the client without your judgment.

## Two roles, one engagement

| The board | The OCM lead |
|---|---|
| **Analyzes** context, data you provide, and options | **Decides** which recommendation to adopt, adapt, or reject |
| **Drafts** first versions of deliverables in a standard format | **Delivers** refined work to the client and sponsor |
| **Challenges** your reasoning, assumptions, and evidence | Owns **judgment**, **client relationships**, and **decisions** |

The board accelerates analysis and strengthens thinking. If it is doing anything in the right-hand column, stop and reset.

> **Tip:** Ask, "Who made the call?" If the honest answer is "the board," you have crossed the line.

## The two-way flow

Advice flows from the board to you: recommendations with rationale and labeled assumptions, first drafts, challenge, risks, and executive-ready language. Decisions, direction, and validated facts flow back:

- **Decisions:** "We are going with option B. Update the training plan to match."
- **Direction:** "Focus on the finance function first."
- **Validated facts:** "The sponsor confirmed three deployment waves, not two. Wave 1 is [BU name] in [country]."

Without this return flow, advisors drift on assumptions. New learning goes into the Client Change Context Brief so every advisor shares a current picture (Chapter 6, "The Five-Step Workflow").

> **Watch out:** Validated facts come from real sources: client data, stakeholder conversations, sponsor confirmation. A fact the board suggested and you did not check is still an assumption.

## The three steps

### Step 1: The human frames the problem

Framing drives output quality. Include the question or deliverable, the relevant Context Brief fields, constraints, what you know and do not know, and who the output is for.

```text
Context: [paste relevant Context Brief fields].
Question: I need a first-draft stakeholder map for the [program name] ERP program, focused on the finance function.
Known: [list confirmed sponsors and leaders].
Unknown: informal influencers, union or works council positions in [country].
Audience: my working draft, not client-facing yet.
Please label every assumption and ask me up to five questions before you draft.
```

### Step 2: The board advises and drafts

The Board Chair routes your request to the right advisors, who respond in the standard format (Recommendation, Rationale, Analysis, Deliverable, Risks, Decisions required, Success measures, Next actions, Board consultation), including only sections that add value. Expect labeled assumptions or targeted questions and no invented facts.

### Step 3: The human decides and delivers

You validate against real data and stakeholders, refine, and present in your own voice. Before you deliver, check:

- [ ] Every assumption is validated or still clearly labeled.
- [ ] No numbers, names, commitments, or quotes came from the board without a real source.
- [ ] The output matches other deliverables and the current Context Brief.
- [ ] You can defend every recommendation without saying "the board said so."
- [ ] Decisions and new facts have been fed back to the board.

## Decision rights

Where the table says "advises," the board's role ends at recommendation.

| Decision | OCM lead | Board | Client / sponsor |
|---|---|---|---|
| Problem framing and which advisors to consult | Decides | Advises (Chair suggests routing) | Not involved |
| Content of draft deliverables | Decides and refines | Drafts | Reviews |
| Stakeholder engagement approach | Recommends | Advises | Approves |
| Readiness rating and go/no-go | Recommends | Analyzes and challenges | Decides |
| Business case, benefits, and targets | Facilitates | Structures and challenges | Owns and decides |
| Leader messages and commitments | Drafts and recommends | Drafts in provisional voice | Approves |
| Scope, timeline, and deployment waves | Validates and records | Flags inconsistencies | Decides |
| What client data the board may see | Decides, within client policy | Not involved | Sets policy |

The board never "decides." On go/no-go, benefits, and scope, you recommend and the client decides.

## What never gets delegated

1. **Final judgment on recommendations.** Only you choose.
2. **Client relationships.** The board helps you prepare; it cannot hold the relationship.
3. **Validation with real stakeholders.** Personas, maps, and impact assessments are hypotheses until real people confirm them.
4. **Facts about the client.** Baselines, benefits, readiness scores, survey results, and commitments come from the client.
5. **What reaches the client.** Every document, message, and slide passes your review.
6. **Decisions about client data.** You decide what the board sees, within client policy. Protect client data.
7. **Ethical and political calls** on resistance, sensitive groups, or leadership conflict.

> **Watch out:** The common slip is presenting a buried, unlabeled assumption as fact. Read every draft as if a sponsor will ask, "Where did this come from?"

## Signs you are over-relying on the board

Review weekly. If two or more apply, rebalance before your next client deliverable.

| Sign | What to do |
|---|---|
| Board drafts go to the client with light edits | Rewrite the summary and recommendation in your own words |
| Facts cannot be traced to a client source | Audit the deliverable; relabel or validate each claim |
| You consult the board before forming any view | Write your view first, then ask the board to challenge it |
| The Context Brief has not been updated in weeks | Update it with every decision and validated fact |

## Professional growth through the board

Faster professional growth for the lead is one of the board's three core outcomes, but only if you use it deliberately.

- **Form a view first.** Write your recommendation in two or three sentences, then compare. The gaps are your learning.
- **Study the challenge questions.** The Operational Realist doubts unsupported green status; the Commercial Challenger pushes from activity to outcomes. Ask those of your own work first.
- **Work your weaker disciplines** from the Chapter 1 self-rating, and keep a short learning log of what each advisor saw that you missed.

| Period | Focus | Practice |
|---|---|---|
| First [2–4] weeks | Learn the board | Solo mode on your strongest discipline, so you can judge output quality |
| Next [4–8] weeks | Fill gaps | Advisors in your weaker disciplines; form a view first every time |
| Ongoing | Build judgment | Board challenge on your own recommendations before every executive decision point |

> **Example:** On a fictional global ERP program, a lead drafts a "ready" Wave 1 recommendation based on training completion. The Business Readiness & Operational Transition Expert asks what demonstrated capability exists. She adds [simulation or dress-rehearsal results] to her go/no-go criteria, and next time asks that question herself.

## Before you move on

- [ ] You can state the operating idea: the board analyzes, drafts, and challenges; you decide and deliver.
- [ ] You know which decisions are yours, which are the client's, and that none are the board's.
- [ ] You have a growth practice you will start this week.
